The Engineering Design Process PLTW

Six steps. Used by every professional engineer. Used in this class all year.

1
Define Problem

Understand the need. Interview the client. Ask "why" until you reach the real problem.

2
Generate Concepts

Brainstorm multiple solutions before committing to one. Quantity first, quality second.

3
Develop Solution

Select the best concept. Sketch it in detail. Evaluate against criteria using a decision matrix.

4
Construct & Test

Build a prototype. Test it against your written criteria. Collect data, not opinions.

5
Evaluate Solution

Did it meet the criteria? What failed? What would you change? Compare results to the design statement.

6
Present Solution

Communicate your design, results, and learnings. Real engineers document everything.

📄 PLTW Reference: Design Process

It's a Cycle, Not a Checklist

What this means in practice

  • You can — and should — loop back to earlier steps
  • Testing reveals a flaw in your definition → go back to Step 1
  • Building shows your concept was wrong → back to Step 2
  • Each loop is called an iteration
  • More iterations = better final design

The professional version

Boeing 737 MAX had thousands of design iterations over decades before it flew. Your motor mount will have 3–5. Same process, different scale.

Step 1 — Define the Problem

The most important step. Also the most skipped.

What it means

  • Understand who has the problem and why
  • Separate the symptom from the real issue
  • Define what "solved" looks like in measurable terms
  • Ask questions before generating any solutions

Common mistake

Jumping straight to solutions before understanding the problem. This is what happened with the spaghetti tower — everyone started building before defining what "good" actually meant.

Tools for Step 1

  • Five Whys — drill to the root cause
  • Problem statement — one sentence describing the need
  • Design statement — measurable criteria + constraints
  • Client interview — ask before you assume

The Five Whys PLTW 0.1.2

What it is

A technique for finding the root cause of a problem by asking "why" repeatedly until you reach something actionable.

Usually takes 3–5 whys to get past the surface symptom.

Why it matters

If you solve the surface problem, the real problem remains. A team that designs a better alarm clock to wake someone up hasn't solved why they're tired — they've just made the symptom slightly less annoying.

"The drawer doesn't close."

— surface symptom

↓

"The side panels were cut without subtracting the saw's kerf, so the box interior is 3mm too narrow."

— root cause (fixable)

Five Whys — Worked Example PLTW 0.1.2

Initial problem statement: "Your friend forgets to study for tests."

Why did you forget about the test?

I had another assignment due the same day.

Why didn't you schedule time for both?

I had soccer practice until 5:00 that night.

Why didn't you study after practice?

The team went to dinner together after.

Why didn't you study after dinner?

I stayed up gaming the night before and was too tired.

Revised problem statement: Your friend needs to manage screen time and sleep schedules so he has the energy to handle multiple responsibilities on demanding days.

Problem Statement vs. Design Statement PLTW

Problem Statement

Describes the situation — who needs what and why.

Written before any solution exists.

"L.E.O.'s drive wheels have exposed hub bolts that catch on field obstacles and create a snag hazard during competition runs."

Design Statement

Describes the solution requirements — measurable criteria + hard constraints.

Written after you've defined the problem.

"Design a hub cap (C1) with a press-fit diameter of 22mm, that fully covers the exposed hub hardware flush with the wheel face, installs without tools, survives a 10N pull-out force, and prints in PLA in under 30 minutes."

The problem statement tells you what needs to happen. The design statement tells you how you'll know if you succeeded.

Criteria vs. Constraints

Both live inside the design statement. They mean different things.

Criteria

What the solution should do — desired outcomes. Can be ranked by priority.

  • Holds all 6 components securely
  • Passes the drop test
  • Fits within the 200×200mm footprint
  • Components are accessible for wiring
  • Holds 6+ tools without contact between them
  • Items visible and grabbable one-handed
  • Fits within a 300mm-wide wall section
  • Looks finished — no visible fastener heads
GOALS MEASURABLE CAN BE RANKED

Constraints

What the solution must work within — limits. Non-negotiable.

  • Cardboard and tape only
  • Must be built in 45 minutes
  • Cannot be taped to the table
  • Only materials provided by Mr. B
  • Hardwood or plywood from the materials bin only
  • Complete in one 50-minute class period
  • Only tools available in the shop
  • Max depth: 80mm off the wall
LIMITS NON-NEGOTIABLE BINARY

Memory shortcut: Criteria = Goals  |  Constraints = Limits
