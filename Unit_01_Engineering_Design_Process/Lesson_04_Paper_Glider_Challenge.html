Lesson 1.4

Same Idea. Different Approach.

Lesson 1.2 — Spaghetti Tower

  • No process — just build
  • Goal was vague until someone measured
  • Failures were unrecoverable
  • Hard to know what to improve
  • Results felt like luck

Today — Glider with EDP

  • Define the problem first
  • Set measurable criteria before building
  • Sketch multiple options, pick one
  • Test against the criteria you wrote
  • Evaluation is actionable — not "it was bad"

By the end of class, you'll know if the process changes anything.

Step 1 — Define the Problem EDP Step 1

Before touching paper, we need a problem statement — together.

Draft Problem Statement (class activity)

Template:

A student needs a way to _____________ using only _____________ so that _____________.

Good problem statements...

  • Describe the need, not the solution
  • Mention who has the problem
  • Say why it matters
  • Don't mention paper airplanes or folding

Example (write yours on the board)

"A student needs to launch a lightweight projectile as far as possible using only one sheet of paper so that they can demonstrate aerodynamic design principles."

Write Your Design Statement

Choose your mission. Then set your criteria and constraints.

Option A

Distance

Farthest flight from throw line. Measure in cm where it lands.

Option B

Accuracy

Land in a target zone on the floor. 3 attempts, score each.

Option C

Hang Time

Longest time airborne. Measured in seconds from throw to landing.

Your Criteria (goals)

Write 2–3. Example:
"Must fly at least 5 meters" or "Must land within 1m of target"

Your Constraints (limits)

  • 1 sheet of 8.5×11 paper only
  • 5-minute build time
  • Folds, no paper wads, full sheet
  • No scissors, no tape, no paperclips
  • Must be thrown — not dropped

Write your design statement in your notebook before the next slide.

Step 2 — Generate Concepts EDP Step 2

Sketch 3 different designs

  • Each should be meaningfully different
  • Think about wing shape, nose type, body width
  • Don't fold — just draw and annotate
  • Label each with your predicted strength

Brainstorming rule

Quantity first. Don't evaluate while generating. You'll pick one in the next step.

Design variables to explore

  • Wing span — long vs. short
  • Nose — pointed vs. blunt
  • Wing angle — flat vs. angled up
  • Body width — narrow vs. wide
  • Folds — simple vs. layered

5 minutes to sketch. Then we pick one and build.

Step 3 — Develop Your Solution EDP Step 3

Choose one design. Commit to it. Write down why you chose it.

In your notebook

  • Circle your chosen design
  • Write 1–2 sentences: why this one over the others?
  • Note any changes you'd make from your sketch

This is the difference

When the spaghetti tower failed, there was nothing to learn from. When this glider underperforms, you'll know exactly what decision led to it — because you documented it.

Decision criteria

Which design scores highest against your criteria? Consider:

  • Which is most likely to achieve your distance/accuracy/hang-time goal?
  • Which can you fold accurately in 5 minutes?
  • Which is most forgiving to small fold errors?

A decision matrix would rank these scores numerically — we'll do that in Unit 2. For now, use your judgment and document it.

Your Materials

Everything you need. Nothing more.

1
sheet of 8.5×11 paper — this is your entire build

No scissors. No tape. No paperclips.

The constraint is intentional. Fewer variables = clearer test results. If every team has different materials, you can't compare results.

Get your sheet when you're ready to start building. Timer starts on the next slide.

BUILD

Fold your chosen design. No deviations.

5:00

click to reset

Step 4 — Construct & Test EDP Step 4

3 flights. Record data every time — don't estimate from memory.

Testing protocol

  • Same throw position for all 3 flights
  • Same throwing style — consistent arm motion
  • Measure from throw line, not from you
  • Partner confirms the measurement

Why consistency matters

If your throw changes between flights, you're testing your arm, not your design. Controlled testing = reliable data.

Record in your notebook

Mission: _______________
Flight 1: _____
Flight 2: _____
Flight 3: _____
Best: _____
Average: _____
Met criteria? Y / N

Step 5 — Evaluate EDP Step 5

Compare your results against what you wrote in Step 1. Not against other teams.

Evaluation questions

  • Did you meet your criteria? Which ones? Which ones fell short?
  • What flight characteristic was the problem — distance, stability, consistency?
  • Which design decision do you think caused it?
  • What would you change on a V2?

Write your evaluation

In your notebook, answer:

1. Did you meet your criteria?
2. What was the biggest failure mode?
3. What is the root cause?
4. What would V2 look like?

If we had time for V2: this is where the cycle loops back to Step 1 or Step 2.

Lesson 1.2 vs. Lesson 1.4

Spaghetti Tower — No EDP

After it fell:

  • "The tape didn't hold"
  • "We should've built taller"
  • "We ran out of time"

None of those lead to a specific fix. They're observations, not decisions.

Paper Glider — With EDP

After evaluation:

  • "Missed distance criterion by 2m"
  • "Nose heavy — stalled after 3m"
  • "V2: wider wings, center fold higher"

These are engineering decisions. You could act on any of them right now.

The process doesn't make you build faster. It makes your failures useful.

Debrief

Discussion Questions

  • Did writing criteria before building change what you built?
  • Did your evaluation feel different from Day 2's debrief? How?
  • Did anyone iterate — build a V2? Was it better?
  • What part of the EDP felt awkward or forced?

The honest question

The kindergartener result from Day 2 — that was about prototyping fast. The EDP isn't the opposite of that. It's about knowing what to prototype and why it should be different next time.
