Prototypes

What is a prototype?

A prototype is an early, often rough version of a design built to test whether an idea actually works — before committing full time and materials to the final build.

Prototypes are intentionally imperfect. Their job is to reveal problems cheaply, when changes are still easy to make.

Good reasons to prototype

Test if the basic shape or structure works

Find weaknesses before using good materials

Try an idea quickly without full commitment

Prototyping in this unit

Folding a rough test glider before the final version

Stacking a quick spaghetti test before building the full tower

Sketching a concept before committing to a design

Criteria & Constraints

Criteria

Performance goals — what you want your solution to achieve.

Chosen by the design team based on what the client needs. You can usually do better than the minimum.

Examples: must hold as much weight as possible; should fly as far as possible

Constraints

Non-negotiable limits imposed from outside — not chosen by the team.

Set by rules, the client, budget, physics, or available materials. Binary: you either violated them or you didn't.

Examples: budget cannot exceed $10; must use only materials from the kit

Quick test

Did the team choose this, or was it imposed on them? → Imposed from outside = constraint.

Can the design do better than the minimum? → If more is always better, it's a criterion (taller = better; faster = better).

Trade-Offs

What is a trade-off?

In engineering, you rarely get everything you want. Improving one quality of a design often makes another quality worse. This tension is called a trade-off.

Trade-offs are not failures — they are decisions engineers make deliberately and defend with reasoning.

Trade-offs from this unit

  • Wider spaghetti tower base → more stable, but less height
  • More cross-bracing → stronger joints, but uses material that could add height
  • Longer glider wings → more lift, but more drag too

How to recognize a trade-off

You're looking at a trade-off when: making one thing better makes something else worse — and there's no way to have both without giving something up.
