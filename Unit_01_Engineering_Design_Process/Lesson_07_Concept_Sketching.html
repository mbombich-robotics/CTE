Why Sketch Before You Model?

Sketching is free

A sketch takes 5 minutes to draw and 1 second to throw away. A bad Fusion 360 model costs 45 minutes. A printed part that doesn't fit costs a print slot that someone else could have used.

Sketching forces thinking

You can't model something in 3D without understanding what it looks like from every angle. Drawing it by hand first forces you to think about all three dimensions before you touch a computer.

Three concepts, not variations

Your three sketches must be meaningfully different from each other — different geometry, different approach. Three versions of the same hub shape with slightly different wall thickness does not count.

What the decision matrix does

It prevents you from picking the design you like and ignoring the one that actually meets your criteria. The matrix makes the decision objective and justifiable.

Professional engineers at every level sketch on paper before they model. The ones who skip this step spend more time fixing broken models than building new ones.

Rules of Concept Sketching

All six rules apply. There are no exceptions.

1

Hand-drawn only

No screenshots from YouTube, no tracing, no printing. Your pen on paper.

2

At least 2 views

Show the component from the front AND top (or isometric). One view is never enough to understand a 3D part.

3

Label every dimension

Every critical dimension from your design brief must appear on the sketch. No unlabeled shapes.

4

Label every feature

Name what each part does: "bore for motor shaft," "spoke," "flange," "bearing pocket," etc.

5

Three genuine concepts

Each concept must differ in a meaningful way — geometry, approach, or feature arrangement. Not just different wall thickness.

6

Neat enough to read

A sketch does not have to be art. It has to be readable by someone other than you. Mr. B cannot approve something he can't interpret.

The Decision Matrix

Evaluate each concept against your design criteria — no guessing

A decision matrix scores each concept against the same criteria. You set the criteria, you score each concept. Then you add up the scores and let the data drive the decision.

Score × Weight for each cell. Sum the column. The highest score is your recommended concept. If two are close, pick the one that scored better on the highest-weight criteria.

How to Complete the Matrix

Step 1 — Score each concept

For each criterion, score 1–5 for each concept:

  • 5 — fully meets this criterion
  • 3 — partially meets it
  • 1 — does not meet it

Step 2 — Multiply by weight

Score × Weight = cell value. A criterion with weight 3 matters three times as much as one with weight 1. This is where the math does the work.

Step 3 — Add up each column

Total the weighted scores for each concept. The highest total is your matrix-recommended design choice.

Step 4 — Write your rationale

In 2–3 sentences: which concept did you choose, why does the matrix support that choice, and is there anything the matrix didn't capture that influenced your decision?

If the matrix recommends a different concept than you wanted to pick, you can still argue for your preference — but you have to explain what criterion the matrix undervalued and why. This is how engineering decisions work in real life.

The Approval Gate

No F360 until your sketches are approved

Bring your three sketches and completed decision matrix to Mr. B. He will initial your brief if the sketches are ready. Opening Fusion 360 before approval = starting over.

Mr. B is checking for

  • Three meaningfully different concepts
  • All critical dimensions labeled on each sketch
  • At least 2 views per concept
  • Sketches that satisfy the criteria from your design brief
  • A filled-in decision matrix with a clear winner

Common reasons sketches are sent back

  • Three concepts that all look basically the same
  • Missing dimensions — "I'll figure it out in F360"
  • Only one view shown
  • The design brief criteria don't match what's drawn
  • Decision matrix is empty or has the same score for everything
