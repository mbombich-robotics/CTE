Good Questions vs. Bad Questions

Closed questions produce yes/no answers. Open questions produce requirements you can write down. You want open questions.

✗ Closed

"Does it need to be strong?"

Answer: "Yes." — tells you nothing measurable.

"Is there a size limit?"

Answer: "Yes." — still nothing you can design against.

✓ Open

"What happens if this part fails during a match?"

"What happens if this part fails during a demo or presentation?"

Answer tells you consequences → you can write a testable criterion.

"What are the maximum dimensions at each interface?"

Answer gives you a specific number → that's a constraint.

If an answer doesn't give you something to write in your brief, ask a follow-up: "Can you give me a specific number?" or "How would you test that?"

Watch First

Before we start, one exchange — so you can see what note-taking looks like.

Student: "What does the hub cap need to interface with, and what are the fixed dimensions at those interfaces?"

Mr. B: "The bore has to press-fit onto the hub post — that post is 22mm diameter. The cap face needs to sit flush with the wheel face when it's installed, and you need clearance for the 6 M3 bolts around the hub edge. That's the fixed stuff. How the outside looks — the profile, any surface detail — that's completely up to you."

→ Three brief entries from one answer: dimension constraint (22mm press-fit bore), geometry criterion (flush with wheel face), clearance constraint (clears M3 bolt heads). Everything outside those interfaces is designer-defined — the exterior profile is where your design lives.

Student: "What does the bearing need to fit into, and what are the critical dimensions at that interface?"

Mr. B: "The spinner body needs a press-fit pocket for the MR128 bearing — outer diameter is 12mm, width is 4mm. Design the pocket at 11.8mm diameter so it's a press fit. Depth should be exactly 4mm, and the bearing face needs to sit flush with the top surface of the spinner body."

→ From this one answer: one dimension constraint (11.8mm pocket diameter), one depth constraint (4mm pocket), one geometry criterion (bearing flush with top face). Three brief entries from one question.

Notebook open. Write answers as you hear them — you cannot reconstruct this from memory.

The Interview

Use your notebook questions. Ask them now.

  • Q1What does this component need to do? Describe the function in one or two sentences.
  • Q2What does it physically connect to, and what are the dimensions at each interface?
  • Q3What are the critical dimensions — the ones that absolutely must be correct for the part to work?
  • Q4Are there any geometry requirements? (No overhangs requiring supports, symmetry, specific wall thickness…)
  • Q5How will we test whether my design is successful? What do I measure or check?
  • Q6What constraints am I working within? (Material, print time, iteration budget…)
  • Q7Is there anything I should not do, or a common mistake to avoid?

Ask any questions you added. If an answer is vague, ask: "Can you give me a specific number?" or "How would you test for that?"

Notes → Constraints

Fill Section 4. Constraints are the non-negotiable limits — things that are fixed regardless of which design concept you choose.

How to spot a constraint

  • It's a hard limit — no workaround
  • Breaking it makes the design fail outright
  • It's binary: either you meet it or you don't
  • Common sources: material, dimensions, time, method, rules

Constraint vs. Criterion

If you can score it on a scale of 1–5, it's a criterion. If it's pass/fail, it's a constraint.

Criterion: "Survives a 10N pull-out force" — you could score a 3N result vs. a 12N result.

Constraint: "Must print in PLA" — it either does or it doesn't.

Criterion: "Spins for at least 30 seconds" — you can compare 20 s vs. 45 s on a scale.

Constraint: "Bearing pocket must be 11.8mm diameter" — the bearing either fits or it doesn't.

Aim for at least 3 specific constraints. "Must look good" is not a constraint — that's a criterion (if you can measure it at all).

Design Statement

You now have everything you need. The design statement is one sentence that ties it all together — user, problem, what success looks like, and the limits you're working within.

"Design a [component] for [client/end user] that [addresses the problem],
meets [key criterion] and [key criterion],
within [constraint] and [constraint]."

It should include

  • Who it's for (from S1)
  • What problem it solves (from S2)
  • At least one criterion reference (from S3)
  • At least one constraint reference (from S4)

It should NOT include

  • A specific design solution ("I will use…")
  • Vague language ("good quality," "as needed")
  • Anything you haven't confirmed with your client

10 minutes — draft it now. If you don't finish in class, it's the first thing tomorrow.
