What Mechanical Advantage Really Means

A machine cannot create energy. But it CAN let you trade distance traveled for force applied — or vice versa. Mechanical advantage quantifies that trade.
  • Effort Force (F_E) — the force YOU apply (input)
  • Resistance Force (F_R) — the force the machine applies to the load (output)
  • Mechanical Advantage (MA) = F_R / F_E
  • MA > 1: Output force > input force. You exert LESS force but must move FARTHER. Example: crowbar lifts a heavy rock — you push down a long distance to lift a short distance.
  • MA < 1: Output force < input force. You exert MORE force but the load moves farther than you do. Example: tweezers give precision movement at the cost of more effort.
  • Conservation of energy always holds: F_E × d_E = F_R × d_R (if no friction)

IMA — Ideal Mechanical Advantage

IMA assumes a perfect machine with no friction. It's calculated from the geometry of the machine alone — no force measurements needed.
  • Lever: IMA = effort arm length / resistance arm length — the longer the effort arm relative to the resistance arm, the greater the mechanical advantage
  • Inclined Plane: IMA = slope length / rise height — a longer, gentler ramp has higher IMA
  • Wheel and Axle: IMA = radius of wheel / radius of axle — large wheel, small axle = high IMA
  • Pulley systems: IMA = number of rope segments supporting the load (covered in L8.4)
  • IMA is always ≥ 1 for a machine that provides force multiplication (and exactly 1 for a fixed pulley that only changes direction)

AMA — Actual Mechanical Advantage

AMA is what you measure in the real world using actual force measurements. It always falls SHORT of IMA because friction steals some energy.
  1. Measure the resistance force (F_R): use a spring scale or force sensor at the load end
  2. Measure the effort force (F_E): use a spring scale at the effort end while the machine is moving
  3. Calculate: AMA = F_R / F_E
  4. Compare to IMA: AMA is always less than IMA (unless the machine is theoretically perfect)
  5. Efficiency from MA: η = AMA / IMA × 100% — tells you what percent of the effort goes to useful work
The gap between IMA and AMA is caused by friction at joints, bending of flexible members, and internal resistance in components. Lubricating bearings and improving surface finish both raise AMA.

Lever Worked Examples (POE_112)

Example 1: Wheelbarrow

Situation: a wheelbarrow holds 240 lb of soil. Effort arm = 6 ft (handle), resistance arm = 2 ft (wheel axle to load center).

IMA = 6 ft / 2 ft = 3

Effort needed (ideal): F_E = F_R / IMA = 240 / 3 = 80 lb

The wheelbarrow lets you lift 240 lb with only 80 lb of effort!

Note: the wheelbarrow is a Class 2 lever (load between fulcrum and effort).

Example 2: Lever Classes

Class 1: Fulcrum between effort and load. Example: seesaw, crowbar, scissors. IMA can be >1, =1, or <1 depending on arm lengths.

Class 2: Load between fulcrum and effort. Example: wheelbarrow, nutcracker. IMA always >1.

Class 3: Effort between fulcrum and load. Example: tweezers, fishing rod. IMA always <1 — speed and reach, not force.

Remember: lever class tells you where the fulcrum is. Class 1 = fulcrum in the middle. Class 2 = load in the middle. Class 3 = effort in the middle.

Inclined Plane Worked Example (POE_112)

  1. Situation: an ADA wheelchair ramp rises 3 feet (height). The slope runs along a 1:12 ratio.
  2. Slope length = height × 12 = 3 × 12 = 36 feet
  3. IMA = slope length / height = 36 / 3 = 12
  4. Interpretation: to roll a 120 lb wheelchair user up this ramp, a caregiver needs only 120 / 12 = 10 lb of force (ideal case)
  5. AMA: in reality, friction on the ramp surface means you'll need slightly more — perhaps 13–15 lb
  6. Trade: you need 10 lb of force, but you push the chair 36 feet instead of lifting it 3 feet straight up
The 1:12 ramp ratio is required by the Americans with Disabilities Act for all public wheelchair ramps. It's an engineering standard derived from mechanical advantage requirements.

Connecting MA to Efficiency

  • Efficiency formula: η = (AMA / IMA) × 100%
  • Alternatively: η = (useful output energy / total input energy) × 100%
  • These are equivalent — both express "what fraction of your effort actually moved the load"
  • Lubrication increases AMA → higher efficiency (same effort force, more load lifted)
  • Surface roughness decreases AMA → lower efficiency (friction turns effort into heat, not useful work)
  • A machine with η = 75% means 75 cents of every energy dollar goes to the load; 25 cents goes to friction/heat
No machine is 100% efficient. Even the smoothest ball bearings have some friction. "Frictionless" is a useful approximation for calculating IMA, but real machines always need AMA measurements to know actual performance.
