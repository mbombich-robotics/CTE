The Wedge as a Simple Machine

A wedge converts a horizontal push (the door) into a vertical force on the floor. The ratio of those forces is its Mechanical Advantage.
  • MA = L ÷ h — length along the floor divided by max height. No units.
  • MA = 7 means every 1 N from the door creates 7 N pushing down on the floor
  • That downward force is what friction has to work with — more floor force = more grip
  • A shallower wedge (smaller angle, longer for its height) has higher MA
  • This is why angle matters more than it looks — a few degrees changes everything
For a notched design (flat toe, doesn't taper to a point), use the taper angle from your CAD file:
MA = 1 ÷ tan(θ)

Notched Designs — The Virtual Apex

  • A notched doorstop has a flat toe — the inclined face doesn't reach the floor
  • If you use the physical length in MA = L/h, you get a lower, incorrect answer
  • The inclined face would reach the floor at the virtual apex — farther back than the physical part
  • The CAD angle θ captures the real incline geometry
  • MA = 1 ÷ tan(θ) uses the effective virtual length automatically

Example: L=100 mm, h=25 mm, θ=8°

L/h = 100/25 = 4.0  ← physical only

1/tan(8°) ≈ 7.1  ← correct (use this)

The virtual apex is where the inclined face would reach the floor

Force Balance — What the Wedge Actually Does

  • Fdoor — door pushes horizontally (trying to eject the wedge)
  • Nfloor — floor pushes straight up on the wedge
  • f — friction acts horizontally, resisting ejection

The inclined surface converts horizontal force into vertical:

Nfloor = Fdoor × MA

Nfloor = Fdoor ÷ tan(θ)

A shallower wedge → higher MA → more floor force → more friction available

Self-Locking — The Math

A wedge is self-locking when friction alone can resist ejection, no matter how hard the door pushes. Let's find out when that's true.
Condition needed:   f ≥ Fdoor friction must resist the door μ · Nfloor ≥ Fdoor μ · Fdoor / tan(θ) ≥ Fdoor μ · Fdoor / tan(θ) ≥ Fdoor ← F_door cancels! μ ≥ tan(θ)     ← self-locking condition MA ≥ 1 / μ      ← equivalent design rule
Fdoor cancels completely. A harder push creates proportionally more floor friction and more ejection force — they scale together. Whether the wedge holds depends only on shape (θ) and surface (μ), never on how hard the door pushes.

Design Thresholds for PLA Doorstops

Since tan(θ) = 1/MA, the self-locking condition is also: MA ≥ 1/μ. Higher MA (shallower wedge) gives more margin. Use this as your design target.
Floor Surface μ (PLA, approx.) Max Angle θ Minimum MA
Smooth tile (most classrooms) 0.27 ≈ 15.1° ≈ 3.7
Hardwood / laminate 0.33 ≈ 18.3° ≈ 3.0
Carpet 0.50 ≈ 26.6° ≈ 2.0
A wedge with θ = 8° has MA ≈ 7.1 — nearly double the minimum for tile. Plenty of margin. A wedge with θ = 14° has MA ≈ 4.0 — barely self-locking on tile. Marginal. A wedge with θ = 16° — not self-locking on tile. The door can push it out.
