V = I × R

V = I × R

V — Voltage

The electrical "pressure" that pushes current through a circuit. Measured in Volts (V). Think of it as water pressure in a pipe.

I — Current

The flow of electrical charge. Measured in Amperes (A) or milliamperes (mA). Think of it as how much water flows per second.

R — Resistance

Opposition to current flow. Measured in Ohms (Ω). Think of it as the size of the pipe — smaller pipe = more resistance = less flow.

Georg Ohm discovered this relationship in 1827. It's one of the most useful equations in all of electronics — if you know any two values, you can find the third.

This triangle only works when the units are correct: Voltage in Volts, Current in Amperes (not milliamperes), Resistance in Ohms.

Units and Prefixes

  • Voltage: 1 V = 1 Volt. Common values: AA battery = 1.5V, 9V battery = 9V, Arduino pins = 5V or 3.3V
  • Current: 1 A = 1 Ampere. Use mA (milliamperes) for small circuits: 1 A = 1000 mA. LED current ≈ 20 mA = 0.020 A
  • Resistance: 1 Ω = 1 Ohm. Common: 330 Ω, 1 kΩ (= 1,000 Ω), 10 kΩ (= 10,000 Ω)
  • Convert before calculating: If I is in mA, divide by 1000 before plugging into V = I × R
Unit error is the #1 math mistake in electrical circuits. Always write units next to every number. If your calculated current is 0.020 A, also write "= 20 mA" to catch errors.

The Proportional Relationship

More R → Less I (at fixed V)

  • If you double the resistance while voltage stays the same, current is cut in half.
  • R × 2 → I ÷ 2
  • Example: V = 9V, R = 100Ω → I = 90 mA. Now R = 200Ω → I = 45 mA.
  • Analogy: narrower pipe = less flow at the same pressure.

More V → More I (at fixed R)

  • If you double the voltage while resistance stays the same, current doubles.
  • V × 2 → I × 2
  • Example: R = 100Ω, V = 5V → I = 50 mA. Now V = 10V → I = 100 mA.
  • Analogy: more pressure = more flow through the same pipe.
These proportional relationships let you predict what will happen before you build. Always ask: "If I change X, what happens to Y?"

Worked Example 1: Find Current (I)

  1. Known: V = 12 V, R = 470 Ω
  2. Want: I = ?
  3. Formula: I = V / R
  4. Calculate: I = 12 / 470 = 0.0255 A
  5. Convert: 0.0255 A × 1000 = 25.5 mA
  6. Check units: Volts / Ohms = Amperes ✓
  7. Sanity check: 25.5 mA is a reasonable current for an LED circuit — not too high, not too low. ✓
Always do a sanity check. If you calculate 500 A flowing through a 9V circuit, something is wrong (maybe you forgot to convert mA to A). Real currents in small electronics are typically in the mA range.

Worked Example 2: Find Resistance (R)

  1. Scenario: You want an LED to draw exactly 15 mA from a 5V supply. The LED's forward voltage is 2V.
  2. Voltage across resistor: VR = Vsupply − Vf = 5 − 2 = 3 V
  3. Known: VR = 3 V, I = 15 mA = 0.015 A
  4. Formula: R = V / I
  5. Calculate: R = 3 / 0.015 = 200 Ω
  6. Pick nearest standard value: 200 Ω is a standard value ✓ (or use 220 Ω for a tiny bit less current)
  7. Verify: I = 3 / 200 = 15 mA ✓

Lab: Verify Ohm's Law

Procedure

  • Gather 4 different resistors (e.g., 100Ω, 220Ω, 470Ω, 1kΩ), a 9V battery, and your DMM
  • For each resistor: predict I using I = V / R (use measured V from your battery, not 9V)
  • Build the circuit: battery → resistor → back to battery (use a breadboard)
  • Measure actual current with the DMM in series; measure voltage across the resistor
  • Record predicted I, measured I, and percent error for each resistor
The measured battery voltage is almost never exactly 9.00V — it depends on charge state. Always use the MEASURED voltage in your calculations, not the nominal value. This is why Step 1 is to measure V first.
