What Is an LED?

  • 💡 LED = Light Emitting Diode — a semiconductor that emits light when current flows through it in one direction. "Diode" means it only conducts in one direction. Current flowing the wrong way = no light, and possibly damage.
  • ↔️ Polarity matters — the LED has a positive terminal (anode) and a negative terminal (cathode). Connect anode to the higher voltage, cathode to ground. Reversed = no current, no light.
  • 🔴🟢🔵 Color depends on the semiconductor material — red, green, blue, white, IR, UV LEDs all have slightly different electrical characteristics. Most common classroom LEDs: red V_f ≈ 1.8–2.2V, green V_f ≈ 2.0–2.4V, blue/white V_f ≈ 2.8–3.4V.
  • ⚡ LEDs are current-driven devices — more current = more light, but too much current destroys them instantly. Maximum forward current for standard 5mm LEDs is typically 20–30 mA. Operating at 10–20 mA is safe and gives good brightness.

Identifying Anode and Cathode

By the Leads

  • Longer lead = Anode (+)
  • Shorter lead = Cathode (−)
  • This is the most reliable method on a new LED
  • Once leads are trimmed, use the other methods

By the Body

  • Look at the flat side of the plastic lens — that side is the Cathode (−)
  • Inside the LED package, the larger metal cup/anvil is the Cathode
  • The smaller wire post inside is the Anode (+)
Memory trick: Cathode → Clipped (shorter lead). Anode → A for Above (longer lead). Or just remember: flat side = minus = cathode.

Forward Voltage (V_f) and Forward Current (I_f)

  • V_f (Forward Voltage) = the voltage drop across the LED when it's conducting. It's relatively fixed regardless of supply voltage.
  • Typical values: Red ≈ 2.0 V · Green ≈ 2.2 V · Blue/White ≈ 3.2 V
  • I_f (Forward Current) = the current flowing through the LED. This controls brightness.
  • Typical operating range: 10–20 mA (0.010–0.020 A) for standard 5mm through-hole LEDs
  • Maximum I_f: 20–30 mA for most classroom LEDs — exceeding this destroys the LED permanently
The forward voltage V_f is NOT the same as your supply voltage. V_f is the voltage that drops across the LED itself. The rest of your supply voltage must drop across your limiting resistor.

Why Every LED Needs a Limiting Resistor

  1. An LED without a limiting resistor connected to a power supply sees all the supply voltage across it.
  2. Since V_f is roughly fixed, the LED tries to pull whatever current is needed to maintain that voltage — which is unlimited from an ideal supply.
  3. In reality, the current skyrockets in microseconds, the junction overheats, and the LED fails permanently.
  4. A series resistor limits the current by consuming the extra voltage (V_supply − V_f) according to Ohm's Law.
  5. Choose R so that at the desired current (say 20 mA), the voltage across R equals V_supply − V_f.
An LED without a current-limiting resistor is like a garden hose with no nozzle — full pressure, no control. The resistor is the nozzle.

Calculating R — The Limiting Resistor

The formula: R = (V_supply − V_f) / I_f
  1. Identify your supply voltage: e.g., V_supply = 9 V
  2. Look up V_f for your LED color: e.g., red LED → V_f = 2.0 V
  3. Choose your operating current: e.g., I_f = 20 mA = 0.020 A
  4. Calculate: R = (9 − 2.0) / 0.020 = 7 / 0.020 = 350 Ω
  5. Pick the nearest standard value: use a 330 Ω or 360 Ω resistor (standard values). Slightly higher is fine — a bit dimmer but safer.
  6. Verify with Ohm's Law: I = (9 − 2.0) / 330 = 0.0212 A = 21.2 mA ✓ (within range)

Multiple LEDs: Series vs. Parallel

LEDs in Series

  • V_supply must be large enough: V_supply > V_f1 + V_f2 + … + V_fn
  • One resistor limits current for the entire chain
  • If any LED burns out, all go out (one path = one failure point)
  • All LEDs in the chain carry the same current
  • Example: 3 red LEDs (3 × 2V = 6V) + 1 resistor from 9V supply → R = (9 − 6) / 0.02 = 150 Ω

LEDs in Parallel

  • Each LED has its own limiting resistor — do NOT share one resistor for parallel LEDs
  • All LEDs see the same voltage (same brightness)
  • If one LED fails, others keep working
  • Total current = I_f × number of LEDs
Rule: Parallel LEDs always need individual resistors. A shared resistor can't control current for multiple LEDs equally — small V_f differences cause one LED to hog all the current and burn.
