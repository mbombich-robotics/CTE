Local History

Why Vicksburg? Why Here?

The Site

  • Settlers chose locations where water dropped in elevation — free mechanical energy
  • A stream falling over a dam turns a wheel; the wheel turns a millstone or a saw
  • Kalamazoo River area: multiple usable head differences for early mills
  • Water was the original power grid — towns built around it

The Key Insight

  • Height difference = stored energy — same principle as a battery
  • Dam = a way to hold energy until it's needed
  • Millpond = energy storage; the wheel = energy converter
  • This exact logic → modern hydroelectric dams and pumped-storage facilities
Coming up: We're going to use water — pressure, pipes, and tanks — as a mental model for electricity. Every concept in this unit has a direct water equivalent.
The Analogy Starts Here

The Water Tower

  • Off-peak hours — small pump fills the tank slowly (cheap, low-demand electricity)
  • Peak demand — morning showers, commute rush — water flows from the tank under gravity pressure
  • Pressure at the tap = height × 0.43 PSI/ft — a 100-ft tower gives ~43 PSI, enough for normal household use
  • The tank is energy storage — exactly like a battery
  • The pump is oversized for peak if it had to work alone — the tank smooths demand
Question: When do water utilities have the highest demand?
Morning showers, before-work rush, lawn irrigation on hot summer evenings.
The Analogy

Water Pressure → Electricity

Water System Electrical Circuit Unit / Notes
💧 Pressure (PSI) ⚡ Voltage (V) The "push" that drives flow. More pressure → more flow for same pipe size.
🌊 Flow rate (GPM) ⚡ Current (A) How much is actually moving. Measured in gallons per minute / amperes.
🔧 Pipe diameter ⚡ Resistance (Ω) Smaller pipe → less flow for same pressure. More resistance → less current.
⛽ Pump / Dam ⚡ Battery / Generator The source of the pressure / voltage.
🏗️ Water tower ⚡ Capacitor Stores energy, releases it when demand exceeds supply.
Electrons don't flow like water, but the relationships between pressure, flow, and pipe size are mathematically identical. The analogy is exact enough to build real intuition.
What Comes Out of the Wall

Voltage — AC and DC

DC — Direct Current

  • Electrons flow in one direction — always
  • Voltage is constant over time
  • Sources: batteries, USB chargers, solar panels
  • What your microcontroller and LED circuits use

AC — Alternating Current

  • Electrons reverse direction 60 times per second (60 Hz in the US)
  • Voltage follows a sine wave — positive half, then negative half
  • US outlet: 120V RMS — peak is actually ±170V
  • Europe: 230V at 50 Hz — same concept, different numbers
The "120V" rating is the RMS (effective) value — the equivalent DC voltage that delivers the same power. The actual peak of your wall outlet is ±170V.
Safety Concept

Earth Ground

  • "Ground" = the earth itself — a massive conductor sitting at 0 V by definition
  • Electrical systems tie one wire to earth so everything else has a known reference point
  • Your house has a ground rod driven into the soil — the green wire in every outlet connects to it
  • If a live wire touches a metal appliance case, fault current flows to earth through the ground wire — not through you
  • Lightning rods, neutral wires, equipment grounds — all using the same principle
Water analogy: Ground is the lowest point in the water system — the drain. Everything flows toward it when there's a path.
The hazard: You standing barefoot on the earth while touching a live wire completes the circuit — you become the path to ground.
The Fundamental Relationship

Ohm's Law — V = I · R

V = I · R
rearranged: I = V / R  |  R = V / I
  • V = voltage (volts) — the pressure
  • I = current (amperes) — the flow rate
  • R = resistance (ohms, Ω) — the pipe restriction
  • Double the voltage → double the current
  • Double the resistance → half the current
  • Straw-sized pipe + high pressure = still a trickle
Electrical Safety

Can 120V Kill You?

  • Voltage doesn't kill — current does. Voltage is the pressure that drives current through you.
  • Your body's resistance is the variable: dry skin ≈ 100,000 Ω, wet skin ≈ 1,000 Ω
  • 120 V ÷ 100,000 Ω = 1.2 mA — a tingle, not lethal (dry)
  • 120 V ÷ 1,000 Ω = 120 mA — easily lethal (wet)
  • Sweat, water, damaged skin — all lower your resistance dramatically
Parents warned you about the outlet — and they were right. But the real danger is wet hands + live wire, not dry contact with low-amperage sources.

Why Path Matters

Current Path Through the Body

Current travels through one arm, torso, and one leg — misses the heart (less dangerous)

Current travels arm-to-arm — directly through the heart. This is why electricians keep one hand in their pocket when probing live circuits.

"Left hand in pocket" — a real rule in industrial electrical work. Keeps the left-hand-to-right-hand path broken and reduces cardiac risk.
Applied Safety

Lightning — Step Potential

  • A lightning strike pushes 100,000+ volts into the ground at the strike point
  • Voltage fans outward — the ground surface has a voltage gradient away from the strike
  • Step potential: the voltage difference between your two feet — the farther apart, the larger the difference
  • Feet together → smaller step → less current through your legs
  • Worst place: open field or hilltop — you become the tallest object, attracting the leader
  • In a car: the metal body acts as a Faraday cage — current travels the shell, not through you
If caught in the open: crouch low, feet together, don't lie flat. Get to a substantial building or a hard-topped vehicle.
