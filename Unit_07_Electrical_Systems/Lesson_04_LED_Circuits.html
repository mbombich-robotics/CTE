What is an LED?

LED = Light Emitting Diode

  • A semiconductor diode that emits light when current flows in one direction (Anode → Cathode).
  • Acts like an electrical check valve: blocks reverse current flow.
  • Current-Driven: Brightness increases with current, but excessive current will instantly burn out the component.
  • Standard 3mm and 5mm through-hole LEDs operate safely at 10 mA to 20 mA (0.010 A – 0.020 A).

Identifying Anode (+) & Cathode (−)

Anode (+) — Positive

  • Longer Lead (on new un-trimmed LEDs)
  • Smaller internal wire post
  • Connects towards higher supply voltage

Cathode (−) — Negative

  • Shorter Lead ("Clipped = Cathode")
  • Flat edge on the plastic lens
  • Larger internal metal anvil flag
  • Connects towards Ground (GND / 0V)

Memory Trick: Flat side = Minus = Cathode

Forward Voltage (Vf) & Forward Current (If)

Forward Voltage (Vf): The fixed voltage drop across the LED junction when conducting.

  • 🔴 Red LED: Vf ≈ 1.8V – 2.2V (typ. 2.0V)
  • 🟢 Green LED: Vf ≈ 2.0V – 2.4V (typ. 2.2V)
  • 🔵 Blue / White LED: Vf ≈ 2.8V – 3.4V (typ. 3.2V)

Forward Current (If): Target operating current (typically 20 mA = 0.020 A).

Note: Supply voltage (Vsupply) is NOT Vf. The extra voltage drops across the limiting resistor!

Calculating Current-Limiting Resistors

Formula derived from Ohm's Law (R = V / I):

R = (Vsupply − Vf) / If

Wiring Multiple LEDs: Series vs. Parallel

Series Circuit

  • LED voltages add up: Vtotal = Vf1 + Vf2 + ...
  • Uses ONE shared resistor for the chain.
  • Same current flows through all LEDs.
  • If one LED fails, all go dark.

Parallel Circuit

  • Each LED gets its OWN limiting resistor.
  • Never share a resistor in parallel (causes thermal runaway / current hogging).
  • All LEDs receive full supply voltage.
  • If one LED fails, others stay lit.
