When Series/Parallel Isn't Enough

Series and parallel rules work when the circuit can be simplified to a single R_eq. But some real circuits have branches that can't be collapsed using those formulas alone. Kirchhoff's Laws handle ANY circuit topology.
  • Branch — a single two-terminal circuit element (e.g., one resistor, one source)
  • Node — a junction where two or more branches connect. Current splits at nodes.
  • Loop — any closed path in a circuit that starts and ends at the same point
  • Gustav Kirchhoff (1845) stated two laws that are just Conservation of Energy and Conservation of Charge applied to circuits

KCL — Kirchhoff's Current Law

"The sum of all currents entering a node equals the sum of all currents leaving it." — I_in = I_out at every node.
  • Based on Conservation of Charge — electrons don't accumulate at junctions; whatever flows in must flow out
  • Works for any node, any time, any circuit topology
  • At a simple junction of three wires: if 5A enters from the left and 3A leaves upward, then 2A must leave downward
  • Series circuit consequence: there's only one path, so current is identical at every point — KCL at every node confirms this automatically
  • Parallel circuit consequence: I_total = I₁ + I₂ + … — the total current splits into branches at every node

KVL — Kirchhoff's Voltage Law

"The sum of all voltage changes around any closed loop equals zero." — voltage drops and rises must cancel out.
  • Based on Conservation of Energy — you can't gain or lose energy just by traveling around a circuit loop
  • Voltage drop — traveling through a resistor in the direction of current flow: subtract (V = −I×R)
  • Voltage rise — traveling through a source from − to + terminal: add (V = +V_source)
  • If you travel against current through a resistor: it's a rise (+I×R). Against current = uphill
  • Series circuit consequence: the source voltage must equal the sum of all resistor drops — that's KVL
  • Parallel consequence: every branch has the same voltage because they share the same two nodes — KVL around any loop containing two parallel branches gives zero

Applying KCL — Find Unknown Currents

  1. Label all node points in the circuit with letters (A, B, C…)
  2. Label current direction for each branch (assume a direction; if result is negative, current actually flows opposite)
  3. Write KCL for each node: I_in = I_out (or ΣI = 0 with sign convention)
  4. Solve the system of equations for unknown currents
  5. Verify by checking that KCL holds at every other node using your solved currents
Strategy: if the circuit has N nodes, you need N−1 independent KCL equations to solve it. For a simple 2-node circuit, one KCL equation is enough.

Applying KVL — Find Unknown Voltages

  1. Identify all loops in the circuit — trace every unique closed path
  2. Choose a direction to traverse each loop (clockwise or counterclockwise — your choice, be consistent)
  3. As you travel the loop, add voltage rises, subtract voltage drops
  4. Set the sum equal to zero: ΣV = 0
  5. Solve the resulting equation(s) for unknown voltages or currents
For circuits with multiple loops, each loop gives one equation. If you have 3 unknowns, you need 3 equations — combine KCL and KVL equations as needed.

KCL and KVL — The Underlying Reason

KCL Explains Series Current

  • In a series circuit, every node has exactly ONE branch entering and ONE branch leaving
  • KCL at every node: I_in = I_out → same current everywhere
  • The "current is constant in series" rule isn't a separate fact — it's KCL applied to single-path nodes

KVL Explains Parallel Voltage

  • In a parallel circuit, trace a loop through two parallel branches: +V_source − V₁ = 0 (one loop) and +V_source − V₂ = 0 (another loop)
  • Therefore V₁ = V₂ = V_source
  • The "voltage is equal across parallel branches" rule is KVL applied to loops containing parallel branches
Series and parallel rules are just shortcuts for applying KCL and KVL to simple topologies. For complex circuits that don't simplify to pure series/parallel, you use KCL and KVL directly.
