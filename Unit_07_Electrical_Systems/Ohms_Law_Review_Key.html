Part C · Solve It

  1. A 9 V battery is connected across a 450 Ω resistor. What is the current?
    I = V ÷ R = 9 V ÷ 450 Ω → I = 0.02 A (20 mA)
  2. 2 A flows through a 6 Ω heating element. What voltage is across it?
    V = I × R = 2 A × 6 Ω → V = 12 V
  3. A 12 V lamp draws 0.5 A. What is its resistance?
    R = V ÷ I = 12 V ÷ 0.5 A → R = 24 Ω
  4. A 5 V Arduino pin drives current through a 1 kΩ resistor. What is the current in mA?
    1 kΩ = 1,000 Ω · I = 5 V ÷ 1,000 Ω = 0.005 A → I = 5 mA
  5. 20 mA flows through a 220 Ω resistor. What voltage is across the resistor?
    20 mA = 0.02 A · V = 0.02 A × 220 Ω → V = 4.4 V
  6. A sensor needs 10 mA from a 3.3 V supply. What resistance would draw exactly that current?
    10 mA = 0.01 A · R = 3.3 V ÷ 0.01 A → R = 330 Ω
  7. A stalled robot drive motor pulls 30 A from a 12 V battery. What is the motor's resistance when stalled?
    R = 12 V ÷ 30 A → R = 0.4 Ω
  8. A 9 V battery is connected to a 4.7 kΩ resistor. What is the current in mA? (Round to two decimal places.)
    4.7 kΩ = 4,700 Ω · I = 9 V ÷ 4,700 Ω = 0.001915 A → I ≈ 1.91 mA

Common mistake: forgetting to convert mA → A or kΩ → Ω before dividing/multiplying (e.g. #5: 20 × 220 = 4,400 V is wrong).

Part D · Predict It

  1. The voltage stays the same and the resistance doubles. What happens to the current?
    The current is cut in half. I = V ÷ R — a bigger R in the denominator means less current.
  2. The resistance stays the same and the voltage triples. What happens to the current?
    The current triples. Current is directly proportional to voltage when R is constant.
  3. A wire connects + straight to − (about 0 Ω). What happens to the current, and why is it dangerous?
    The current becomes extremely large (a short circuit). Dividing by almost 0 Ω gives a huge current, which makes the wire and battery get very hot — it can melt insulation, damage the battery, or start a fire.
  4. The switch in a circuit is open. What is the current, and what is the resistance of the open gap?
    Current = 0 A; the gap's resistance is (nearly) infinite. Air in the gap blocks the charge, so no current can flow.

Part E · LED Resistors

R = (Vsupply − Vf) ÷ I  ·  Round up to the next standard value: 100, 120, 150, 180, 220, 270, 330, 360, 390, 470, 560, 680 Ω
  1. A red LED (Vf = 2.0 V) runs at 20 mA from a 9 V battery. Calculate R, then pick the standard resistor.
    R = (9 V − 2.0 V) ÷ 0.02 A = 7 V ÷ 0.02 A = 350 Ω → R = 350 Ω · use 360 Ω
  2. A blue LED (Vf = 3.2 V) runs at 15 mA from a 5 V supply. Calculate R, then pick the standard resistor.
    R = (5 V − 3.2 V) ÷ 0.015 A = 1.8 V ÷ 0.015 A = 120 Ω → R = 120 Ω · use 120 Ω (already a standard value)

Rounding up means slightly less current than the target — safer for the LED. Rounding down (e.g. 330 Ω for #1) would push it past 20 mA.
