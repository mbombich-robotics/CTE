Capacitors — Storing Charge

What It Does

  • Stores electrical charge between two conductive plates separated by an insulator (dielectric)
  • Measured in Farads (F) — typical values: µF (microfarad) and pF (picofarad)
  • Voltage builds as charge accumulates; it can't change instantly
  • Releases stored charge back into the circuit when voltage drops

Where You'll See Them

  • Power supply filtering: smooths out voltage ripple after a rectifier
  • Timing circuits: a capacitor charging through a resistor creates a predictable time delay (RC time constant τ = R × C)
  • Decoupling: small caps placed near IC power pins absorb momentary current spikes
  • Polarity warning: electrolytic caps are polarized — reversed voltage destroys them and can cause them to rupture
A capacitor is the charge equivalent of a spring: it resists sudden voltage changes the way a spring resists sudden compression. It's the "anti-voltage-spike" component.

Inductors — Opposing Current Change

What It Does

  • A coil of wire that stores energy in a magnetic field when current flows through it
  • Measured in Henries (H) — typical values: µH to mH range
  • Opposes changes in current: resists current increasing AND resists current decreasing
  • When the current path is suddenly broken, the inductor generates a voltage spike to keep current flowing — this can destroy transistors if unprotected

Where You'll See Them

  • Motors and relays: their coils are inductors — always use a flyback diode across them
  • Filters: inductors block high-frequency signals while passing DC
  • Power converters: switching regulators (boost/buck) use inductors to store and transfer energy
An inductor is the current equivalent of a flywheel: it resists sudden current changes the way a flywheel resists sudden speed changes. It's the "anti-current-spike" component.

Transistors — Controlled Switches

A transistor is a current-controlled switch (BJT) or voltage-controlled switch (MOSFET). A small signal at the control pin allows or blocks a much larger current through the main path.

NPN BJT as a Switch

  • Base — control input. A small current (≈1 mA) here…
  • Collector — high side of the load
  • Emitter — connects to ground
  • When base current flows: transistor saturates → collector–emitter looks like a closed switch → load current flows
  • When base current stops: transistor cuts off → no load current
  • Common for driving motors, relays, and LEDs from microcontroller pins

MOSFET as a Switch

  • Gate — voltage input (no current needed to hold ON)
  • Drain / Source — main current path
  • Gate voltage above threshold → switch ON; below → switch OFF
  • N-channel MOSFETs are the most common in motor drivers
  • Advantage over BJT: nearly zero gate current → very efficient switching

Common Integrated Circuits (ICs)

555 Timer

One of the most popular ICs ever made. In astable mode it generates a continuous square wave (blink an LED, generate a tone). In monostable mode it produces a single timed pulse. Uses resistors and a capacitor to set timing — RC time constant controls frequency.

Op-Amp (e.g. LM358)

An operational amplifier amplifies the difference between two input voltages. In comparator mode: output goes high when one input exceeds the other — useful for sensors, line followers, level detectors. Thousands of analog circuits are built around op-amps.

Motor Driver (e.g. L298N)

Contains an H-bridge — four transistors arranged to allow current through a motor in either direction. Control pins set forward, reverse, brake, or coast. Most motor drivers also handle the flyback protection internally. Rated by peak current (e.g., 2A per channel).

ICs package complex circuits into a single component. You don't need to know every internal transistor — you need to know the pinout, supply voltage range, and what each pin does. Always read the datasheet.

Magnetics & Electromagnets

Current Creates Magnetic Fields

  • Any wire carrying current is surrounded by a magnetic field — this is how electromagnetism works
  • The field wraps around the wire in circles (right-hand rule: thumb points in current direction, fingers curl in field direction)
  • Winding the wire into a coil (solenoid) concentrates and amplifies the field — the fields from each turn add up
  • Adding an iron core inside the coil multiplies the field strength dramatically (iron is highly permeable)

Practical Uses

  • Relays: an electromagnet pulls a mechanical switch — lets a small signal control a high-power circuit with electrical isolation
  • Solenoids: linear actuators — the core pulls into the coil when energized (door locks, valves)
  • Transformers: two coils share a core — voltage in one coil induces a voltage in the other; ratio of turns sets the voltage ratio
  • Electric motors: rotating version of the electromagnet principle
Faraday's Law (1831): a changing magnetic field induces a voltage. This is the operating principle of every generator, transformer, and motor on the planet.

DC Motors

How a DC Motor Works

  • A coil of wire (the armature) sits inside a permanent magnet
  • Current through the coil creates an electromagnet that is attracted and repelled by the fixed magnets — producing torque (rotational force)
  • A commutator and brushes switch the current direction continuously so the motor keeps spinning rather than locking to one position
  • Reversing the supply voltage reverses the rotation direction

Key Motor Specs

  • Stall current: the maximum current drawn when the motor is held still — can be 5–10× the normal running current. Motor drivers must be rated for stall current or they'll burn out.
  • Back-EMF: a spinning motor generates its own voltage opposing the supply. At full speed, back-EMF limits current; at stall, there is no back-EMF, so current surges.
  • PWM speed control: rapidly switching the motor on and off at varying duty cycles controls average power and speed without wasting energy as heat.
Always add a flyback diode across a DC motor (cathode to + supply, anode to − supply). When the motor is switched off, its inductor kicks back a voltage spike that can destroy the switching transistor.
