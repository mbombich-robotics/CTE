What Is Artificial Intelligence?
You've heard it everywhere — self-driving cars, chatbots, image filters. But what is AI actually doing under the hood? Today we build the vocabulary and the mental model.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to define AI, machine learning, and deep learning; describe the three types of machine learning; explain what training data is and why it matters; and identify where bias can enter an AI system.

Slide 4 of 11
Three Types of Machine Learning
🏷️
Supervised
Learns from labeled examples. You show it thousands of cat photos labeled "cat." It learns to recognize cats in new photos.
🔍
Unsupervised
Finds patterns on its own without labels. Group customers by behavior. No one told it what the groups were.
🎮
Reinforcement
Learns by trying actions and receiving rewards or penalties. How AlphaGo learned to beat world champions.
Teachable Machine — which you'll use on Day 3 — uses supervised learning. You provide labeled examples; it learns from them.
Slide 5 of 11
Supervised Learning — In Depth
How It Works

Labeled Training Data

Every example has a correct answer attached. Input: photo of a stop sign → Label: "Stop Sign." The model adjusts until its predictions match the labels.

Examples

Where You See It

Spam email filters, medical image diagnosis, credit approval, voice recognition, self-driving car object detection, Teachable Machine.

The key insight: the model never sees rules. It only sees examples. Everything it knows about a stop sign it inferred from labeled pictures — not from reading the definition of a stop sign.
Slide 6 of 11
Unsupervised Learning
How It Works

Find Your Own Patterns

No labels provided. The algorithm groups similar inputs together or identifies structure in the data. The engineer then interprets what each group means.

Examples

Where You See It

Spotify recommending songs, Netflix clustering viewers, anomaly detection in cybersecurity, customer segmentation for marketing.

Unsupervised learning is powerful when you don't know what the groups are in advance. No one told Spotify what "indie folk" was — the clustering algorithm found listeners who liked the same songs, and a human later applied the genre label.
Slide 7 of 11
Reinforcement Learning
1
Agent observes its environment state (e.g., game board, traffic scene)
2
Agent takes an action (move a game piece, steer the car)
3
Environment returns a reward (+1 for winning, −1 for crashing)
4
Agent adjusts its strategy to maximize cumulative reward over many tries
AlphaGo Zero learned to beat the world's best Go players using reinforcement learning. It played millions of games against itself — no human examples needed.
Slide 8 of 11
How a Model Learns: Training
1
Feed an input through the model — get a prediction.
2
Measure the error — how far off was the prediction from the correct answer?
3
Adjust the weights — the model nudges thousands of internal numbers to reduce that error.
4
Repeat — millions of times, across thousands of examples. Accuracy improves with each pass.
The internal numbers being adjusted are called weights. A trained model is essentially a huge table of weights. When you "train" in Teachable Machine, you're watching this happen in seconds.
Slide 9 of 11
Neural Networks
Structure

Layers of Nodes

Input layer receives raw data (pixel values, sensor readings). Hidden layers extract patterns — edges, then shapes, then faces. Output layer gives the final prediction.

Why "Neural"?

Loosely Inspired by Biology

Each "neuron" fires based on signals from the previous layer, weighted by learned values. The brain uses a similar scheme — but at vastly greater complexity.

A simple neural network for recognizing handwritten digits might have 784 input nodes (one per pixel), two hidden layers with ~128 nodes each, and 10 output nodes (one per digit 0–9). After training on 60,000 examples, it achieves ~99% accuracy.

Slide 10 of 11
Training Data and Bias
The model learns only from what it is shown. If the training data has gaps, those gaps become the model's blind spots — permanently.
Sources of Bias

Where It Enters

Skewed collection (only certain people photographed), labeling errors, historical patterns baked in, underrepresentation of edge cases.

Real Consequences

Why It Matters

Facial recognition systems with higher error rates for darker skin tones. Hiring algorithms that downscored women's résumés. Criminal risk tools that flagged by race.

You'll explore bias firsthand in the Teachable Machine activity by deliberately training a bad model and observing the failure modes.
