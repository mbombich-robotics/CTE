Teachable Machine

What Is Teachable Machine?
Google's Teachable Machine is a web-based tool that lets you train a real machine learning model by showing it examples through your webcam. No coding required. Behind the scenes it runs a neural network that uses supervised learning — the same type of ML that powers image classification at Google Photos, facial recognition, and medical imaging tools.

What It Can Do

Image classification (camera or uploaded files), sound classification, and body pose detection. Today we're using image classification.

What Happens When You Train

It runs a neural network in your browser, adjusting weights until the model's class predictions match your labeled examples. Training takes seconds because of browser GPU acceleration.

Everything you collected in Lesson 5.1 about supervised learning — labeled examples, training, inference — you are about to experience directly.
Step 1 — Open & Set Up
Getting Started
1
Go to teachablemachine.withgoogle.com
2
Click "Get Started"
3
Choose "Image Project" → then "Standard image model"
4
You'll see a project with two default classes: Class 1 and Class 2. You'll rename these and add a third.
5
Allow camera access when the browser asks.
Don't sign in yet — you don't need a Google account to train a model. You'll just export it locally.
Step 2 — Define Your Classes
Choose What to Classify
Your model needs at least 3 classes. Choose something you can photograph at your desk with clear visual differences. The goal is something simple and testable — here are some ideas:
Option A Thumbs up / Thumbs down / Flat hand — classic and fast to collect
Option B Pen / Marker / Pencil — simple objects at your desk
Option C Your own idea — 3 visually distinct things you can hold up
1
Click the pencil icon next to Class 1 and rename it to your first category.
2
Do the same for Class 2. Then click "Add a class" and name the third.
Step 3 — Collect Training Samples
Feed the Model Examples
For each class, you'll hold down the "Hold to Record" webcam button while showing that class to the camera. The goal is variety — the model needs to handle your subject from different angles and backgrounds.
1
For each class, click "Webcam" then hold down "Hold to Record."
2
Collect at least 60–80 frames per class. Move around slightly while recording — slight angle changes, different distances.
3
Try to keep the same lighting for all three classes — shadows are a major source of false patterns.
Tip: If you collected samples while sitting — also collect a few while leaning slightly forward or backward. Real users won't hold things at exactly the angle you trained on.
Step 4 — Train Your Model
Let the Algorithm Work
1
Click the blue "Train Model" button. A progress bar will run — typically 10–30 seconds.
2
When training finishes, the Preview panel opens on the right. Your webcam feed appears with live class predictions and confidence percentages.
3
Test each of your three classes. Watch the confidence bars — high confidence on the right class is a sign of good training.
What just happened: the browser ran a neural network through thousands of iterations, adjusting weights until predictions matched your labels. You just trained an AI model.
Step 5 — Test and Explore
What Does Your Model Actually Know?
A model that performs well on training data can still fail in unexpected ways on new data. This is called overfitting. Your job now is to find the edges of what your model knows.
→
What happens if you show your class from a very different angle than you trained on?
→
What if you hold it against a different background?
→
What if the lighting changes dramatically?
→
What if you show it something from a completely different category — does it still claim high confidence?
→
What if you show it nothing (point the camera at the ceiling)?
Note any surprising failures. You'll use these observations in your reflection questions.
Step 6 — Deliberately Break It
Design a Bad Training Set
Now you're going to create a second model intentionally trained with biased data — so you can see firsthand how training data shapes what a model believes.
1
Create a new project. Click the Teachable Machine logo to start fresh.
2
Use the same 3 classes, but train one class with only 10 samples and the other two with 80+ samples.
3
Train the model. Test all three classes. Which one fails first?
4
Now also try: collect all samples for one class holding the object at a single fixed position. Notice what happens when you move it.
This is exactly how real-world AI bias works. The model isn't "wrong" — it learned exactly what you showed it. The bias was in the data, not the algorithm.
Reflection — Answer These
Think and Discuss
1. When your model failed during the "test and explore" step, what caused the failure? Was it the algorithm, the training data, or both?
2. When you intentionally trained with imbalanced data, which class suffered? Why does sample count affect model performance?
3. Imagine this model was being used to sort products on a factory assembly line and your "bad" version was deployed. Describe one real consequence.
4. Based on Lesson 5.2 — who would be responsible for harm caused by a model trained on poor data? The data collector? The engineer who deployed it? The company?
5. What would you do differently in a production model compared to what you did here in a 5-minute classroom activity?
Slide 11 of 11 — Extension
Export Your Model
Want to use your trained model in a real web page? Teachable Machine can export it.
1
Click "Export Model" in the top bar.
2
Choose "Tensorflow.js" → "Upload (shareable link)."
3
Teachable Machine will give you a snippet of JavaScript code that loads your model and runs it in any web page.
4
Paste that code into a basic HTML file — open it in Chrome — and your classifier runs live in the browser, using your trained model.

This is how real web apps use ML models. The same export pipeline is used by professional developers integrating image classification into production websites and apps. Next up: Unit 5 Quiz on Day 5 — see your instructor for access.
