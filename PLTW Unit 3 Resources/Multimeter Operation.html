Multimeter Operation

A multimeter is an electrical device that typically measures voltage, current, and resistance. This resource will guide you to set up and use a multimeter to measure voltage and resistance.

Multimeter Function Test

Before relying on multimeter measurements, use the following procedure to verify that the test probes are properly connected to the multimeter and that the multimeter accurately measures at least one measurement.

  1. Set the multimeter rotary dial to the OFF position.

NOTE: Whenever you are not using a multimeter, move the dial to the OFF position.

  1. Connect the black probe to the multimeter port marked COM. COM is an abbreviation for common ground. 

  1. Connect the red probe to the multimeter port marked with a combination of letters and symbols, including the symbol for ohm, Ω. 

  1. Confirm there is not a continuous path of resistance (an open circuit condition) between the probes when the probes are not touching, otherwise the probes might produce a false resistance measurement. Set up the multimeter probes as shown in Figure 4.

    Term

    Definition

    open circuit

    An electrical circuit that has a very high resistance between the voltage source and ground.

    1. Move the multimeter dial to the highest resistance scale.

    2. Verify that the probes are not touching.

    3. Confirm that the resistance measurement shows a very high, infinite resistance.

NOTE: The resistance should read the maximum value possible on the dial, often indicated with an infinity sign, ∞. The multimeter displays a number relative to the maximum reading possible. For the multimeter in Figure 4, the maximum resistance reading is 1, therefore, when the meter is set to the high resistance scale, we consider a 1 to indicate an infinite resistance, or an open circuit. 

  1. Confirm there is a continuous path without resistance (a short circuit ) between the test probes, so the probes do not produce a false resistance measurement. Set up the multimeter probes as shown in Figure 5.

    Term

    Definition

    short circuit

    An electrical circuit that has a resistance close to 0 between the voltage source and ground. 

    1. Move the multimeter dial to the lowest resistance scale.

    2. Hold the tips of the probes to firmly touch.

    3. Confirm that the resistance measurement shows close to 0 resistance.

  1. Remove the probes and turn the rotary dial to OFF.

Multimeter as an Ohmmeter 

Now that you have verified that the multimeter is correctly reading open and short circuits, practice using the multimeter to measure the resistance of a component or part of a circuit.

  1. Move the multimeter rotary dial to the resistance range that is just above the expected resistance measurement. Since the resistance is expected to be 330 Ω, select the 2 kΩ range setting as shown in Figure 6. 

  1. Firmly hold one multimeter probe in contact with a conductive surface at points close to the resistance to be measured. It does not matter which color probe is connected to each wire.

  2. Firmly hold the second multimeter probe in contact with a conductive surface on the opposite side of the resistance to be measured at points close to the resistance.

  3. The resistance value shown on the LCD needs to be scaled within the maximum range selected. For example 0.324 on a 2k range setting is 324 Ω, as shown in Figure 6.

NOTE: The k stands for kilo, or one thousand. The maximum range for the 2k setting is 2000 Ω. Similarly, the maximum range for the 20k setting is 20,000 Ω; and the maximum range for the 200 setting is 200 Ω. 

  1. Remove the probes and turn the rotary dial to OFF.

NOTE: The resistance range must be set with the rotary dial to measure the resistance correctly. If the expected resistance value is not known, then start at the lowest resistance scale, for example 200 Ω on the multimeter. If the value displayed is “1.” as shown in Figure 7, then the resistance measured exceeds the range selected. Use the next higher scale until a valid resistance measurement is displayed. Some multimeters have an auto-ranging function that displays the actual resistance measurement without a need to manually select the range.

NOTE: You can reconfigure the probes to provide a larger conductive surface area while decreasing the diameter of the end of the probes. If needed, slide the probe shields off.

Multimeter as a Voltmeter 

In this section, you will use the multimeter to measure the voltage of a component or section of a circuit.

  1. Move the multimeter rotary dial to the DC voltmeter range that is just above the expected voltage measurement. In Figure 9, the probes of the multimeter are placed to measure the voltage of the power source. Because the power source is set to 3 V, the expected voltage is 3 V; therefore you would select the next higher voltage range setting on the multimeter, 20 V.

  2. Firmly hold the positive multimeter probe in contact with a conductive surface at points close to the more positive side of the voltage to be measured, which is where the positive power supply lead is connected. The polarity of a voltage measurement does matter, so the positive probe should be used.

  3. Firmly hold the negative multimeter probe in contact with a conductive surface on the opposite side of the voltage to be measured at points close to the component.

  4. The value shown on the LCD display needs to be scaled within the maximum range selected. For example 3.14 on a 20 range setting is 3.14 V.

  5. Remove the probes and turn the rotary dial to OFF.

Multimeter as an Ammeter

In this section, you will use the multimeter to measure the current of a component or section of a circuit. 

  1. Move the multimeter rotary dial to the ammeter range that is just above the expected current measurement in Figure 10. Because the power source is set to 3 V and the resistor value is 330 Ω, the expected current is 0.009 A or 9 mA. Therefore, you would select the next highest current range setting on the multimeter, 20m (20 mA).

NOTE: For circuits where the current is expected to be greater than 200 mA, move the red probe to the 10 A probe jack. 

  1. Use the multimeter in place of a current-carrying wire in the circuit to measure the current through the circuit, as shown in Figure 10. 

    1. Remove a wire from the circuit.

    2. Firmly hold the positive multimeter probe in contact with a conductive surface at points close to the more positive side of the circuit. The polarity of a current measurement does matter, so the positive probe should be used.

    3. Firmly hold the negative multimeter probe in contact with a conductive surface on the more negative side of the circuit, where the replaced wire was connected.

  2. The value shown on the LCD display needs to be scaled within the maximum range selected. For example, 9.16 on a 20m range setting is 9.16 mA or 0.00916A. 

  3. Remove the probes and turn the rotary dial to OFF.
