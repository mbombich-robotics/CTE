Technical Writing

Any writing that documents technical information is considered technical communication. If you work in biomedical science or any other technical field, you will need to produce and consume technical communication.

Technical writing differs from creative writing. Creative writing is often used to entertain or elicit emotion and is typically less formal than technical writing. 

The main purpose of technical communication is to clearly and efficiently convey complex information so that the audience can understand and use the information. In addition to presenting technical information, the content may include conclusions and recommendations.

DID YOU KNOW? Technical communication includes text, data, graphs and charts, and technical illustrations. Examples include email, web content, press releases, user guides, proposals, and technical reports.

Technical Communicator

A technical communicator has a solid understanding and practice in their language, in terms of grammar, spelling, syntax, flow, and logical organization of content. The “technical” in technical communicator describes the type of material that they write or edit. Technical communicators use their expertise to produce many types of documentation, including software and hardware user guides, job aids or quick guides, technical reference manuals, technical drawings, reports, training materials, software development guides, charts, presentations—any type of deliverable whose purpose is to convey complex information in clear text and in a logical layout that supports the meaning of the text.

Until about 20 years ago, few college degree programs existed for technical writers or editors. Many of today’s seasoned technical communicators have education and work experience in a wide variety of technical fields. They migrated to technical communication as a logical progression in their own career paths. Some are former engineers and computer scientists. Others majored in English or journalism. 

Some, like Paula Robertson(opens in a new tab), were able to use their love of the language and aptitude for refining details to move into technical communication at entry level and grow it into their life’s work. Today, the profession of technical communication(opens in a new tab) is supported by degree programs at a number of colleges and universities, and a four-year degree is often a requirement for entry-level positions. 

Know the Audience

One of the most important considerations when developing technical documents is the audience. Who will read your technical writing? How will they use the information? Answers to these questions determine what content you include and how you present the information. 

Use the following prompts to help you identify the characteristics, needs, and wants of your target audience.

  • Who is the primary audience? Who will read the content?
  • What is the audience’s level of technical expertise? (highly technical? semi-technical? nontechnical?)
  • If the audience has technical expertise, which field(s) do they have experience in?
  • How will the audience use the content?
    • Read it to gather knowledge and then file or forward the content?
    • Respond to the content?
    • Absorb and apply the content in their own work?
    • Use the content to make a decision?
  • What does the audience want and need to get out of the content?

Researchers often present about their work, discoveries, and findings. A presentation for their peers may look different from a presentation they are giving for the public.

  • Presenting to peers: The audience is highly experienced. The researcher can use technical terms and share implications to the field, including how their peers may implement the information in their own work.
  • Presenting to the public: The audience may have little to no experience, so the researcher should use common terms that are easy to understand. They should summarize the key takeaways of their findings and how they relate to everyday life.

The Writing Process

After you have identified your target audience and the purpose for your technical communication, you can begin the writing process. The writing process typically includes the following steps.

  1. Determine the content and delivery types

    Based on your audience and their needs, decide what type of content you need to develop, such as a notebook entry, technical report, proposal, progress report, or procedure. Choose a format and delivery method that is appropriate for the content type. Refer to Know the Audience.

  2. Conduct research

    Develop a high-level, preliminary outline that identifies the important information you want to present. Then investigate your topic(s). Collect existing information from primary and secondary sources. This can include collecting background information, conducting interviews, performing experiments and collecting data, and taking photos. For specific guidance, see Effective Research(opens in a new tab).

  3. Write an outline

    Develop a working outline. Organize the topics to ensure a logical flow of information. Include everything needed to cover the subject and nothing more. Note that you can revise your outline as you continue to solidify your thinking.

  4. Write a draft

    The purpose of the first draft is to capture your thoughts quickly. As you write, keep in mind your audience and the purpose of the document, but do not spend a lot of time crafting the language. You will return to your work (many times) to revise and improve the writing.

  5. Review and revise

    Read your work multiple times. Each time you read your work, check a different element of your writing. First and foremost, make sure your work is accurate. Recheck all of the facts and verify sources. Then read and revise your work at least three more times to check each of the mechanical elements listed in Tips for Effective Technical Writing. 

    Verify that visual elements are appropriate to clarify and enhance the work—do not use visuals that do not directly support or enhance the content. Make a final pass to be sure the content makes sense, is logically organized, and addresses the original purpose of the writing.

    DID YOU KNOW? This step, also known as editing, should take twice as long as drafting your document.

  6. Have someone else review your work

    Ask a peer to review your writing and provide feedback. If possible, also ask a technical expert to review the content. And it is always a good idea to have a member of the target audience test your work. Have the reviewers check or test the technical accuracy of any procedural steps or instructions. For more guidance, see Providing Peer Feedback(opens in a new tab).

  7. Properly cite all of your sources 

    See Citing Sources(opens in a new tab) for more guidance.

Tips for Effective Technical Writing

Each time you develop technical content, use the following list of tips to help you revise and improve your writing. Note that the intent of the examples is for you to compare the words in blue.

Technicality 

  • Is the writing at an appropriate technical level for the target audience? 
  • Include only the facts. 
  • Do not editorialize (give your opinion).

Voice

Voice indicates whether the subject of a sentence does the action—which is called “active”—or the subject is acted on or is “passive.” Active voice is almost always preferred in instructional design, but passive voice is sometimes acceptable (see Formal Language Style). 

Instead of ThisWrite This
This data can be used by meteorologists to predict weather patterns. Meteorologists can use this data to predict weather patterns. 
A mandatory evacuation has been ordered for your community. The National Weather Service ordered a mandatory evacuation for your community. 

To learn a simple way to recognize passive voice and turn it into active voice, watch the How to Turn Passive Voice into Active video.

Instructional Language Style 

Use instructional language when you write instructions, directions, or procedures. Write directly to the reader. Use second person (the implicit “you”), imperative mood (“Do this action”). Instructional language is in active voice. 

Instead of ThisWrite This
The user should click the Reading icon to open the document. (indirect, impersonal) Good: Click the Reading icon to open the document. (second person, imperative)

Better: To open the document, click the Reading icon. (“why” before “what”) 

Best: To open the document, click Reading. (avoids needless words)

Formal Language Style 

When writing an academic article or formal technical report, use formal language. Use third person—he/him, she/her, they/them, as the subject of a sentence, not first person—I/me, we/us. Formal language can be and often is in passive voice.

The following examples highlight the difference in the subjects of the sentences (in green); these examples apply to formal language only. 

Instead of ThisWrite This
I discovered the micropipette needed to be calibrated before I began the experiment. The researcher discovered the micropipette needed to be calibrated before she began the experiment. 
You should press the red button before you proceed.
The user should press the red button before they proceed. 

Word Choice

  • Avoid contractions. 
  • Check to be sure you use commonly confused words correctly. Some examples:
    • there, their, they’re
    • its, it’s
    • except, accept
    • effect, affect 
    • principle, principal
    • then, than
  • Do not overuse impersonal pronouns—it, that, this; the antecedent to a pronoun (the preceding word(s) that the pronoun refers to) is often difficult to identify. Be explicit even if it means repeating the words.
Instead of ThisWrite This
A variety of treatment options are available for AIP, including a high intake of glucose, intravenous heme therapy, behavior modification, and pain therapy. There still is no cure for it, and the treatments do not guarantee the prevention of future attacks. A variety of treatment options are available for AIP, including a high intake of glucose, intravenous heme therapy, behavior modification, and pain therapy. There still is no cure for AIP, and the treatments do not guarantee the prevention of future attacks. 
In their last annual report, Prosthetic, Inc. claimed that their profits increased significantly more than Orthotics and Prosthetics’ profits. In the report, they posted the following graphs showing their profits...

Conciseness 

Use fewer, more direct words. 

Instead of ThisWrite This
The purpose of this section is to explain the process by which… This section explains the process by which... 
Based on the experiment, the results show that the heart rate will increase an estimated amount of approximately five beats per minute. 
The experimental results show that the heart rate increases about five beats per minute. 

Avoid needless words. Avoid exaggeration with words like very, extremely, actually, and great.

Instead of ThisWrite This
Start out at the corner of... Start at the corner of... 
You should completely eliminate fatty foods from your diet. 
You should eliminate fatty foods from your diet.  
It is extremely important to check the water level.
It is important to check the water level.
The user was really anxious about...
The user was anxious about...
