Effective Research

Research is the process of gathering information from various sources to provide background knowledge for a topic. Your research may include both primary and secondary sources.

Information from individuals directly connected to the data or information. Primary sources are immediate, first-hand accounts of a topic, from people who had a direct connection with it. Primary sources can include: Texts of laws and other original documents, newspaper reports by reporters who witnessed an event or who quote people who did, speeches, diaries, letters and interviews, original research, data sets, survey data, photographs, video, or audio that capture an event.  

May incorporate information from primary sources but include interpretation and analysis. Secondary sources are one step removed from primary sources, though they often quote or otherwise use primary sources. They can cover the same topic, but add a layer of interpretation and analysis. Secondary sources can include: analysis or interpretation of data, scholarly or other articles about a topic authored by people not directly involved, or documentaries.    

Before you start researching a topic, come back to this list of Do’s and Don’ts for Conducting Research. Remember the importance of credibility, the number of sources, and citations. 

DoDon't
Use reputable sources
Reliable sources include books and periodicals, Google Scholar, academic journals, research databases, professional organizations and societies, and government entities.
Examples: Universities, U.S. Patent Office, National Cancer Society, and ASTM International Standards. 		Use sources that are not validated or are hosted in places where the information can be easily changed by anyone. 
Evaluate each source for CRAAP
Use the CRAAP Test to evaluate each source for Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, and Purpose. (See Source Evaluation Criteria below.) 		Use resources that are not current, not relevant, not authoritative, not accurate, or were created for a purpose that may introduce bias or misrepresent information. 
Use multiple sources
Collect information from multiple sources to validate your findings. 		Collect all your information from a single source for your research.

Cite all your references using APA style or another style (MLA or Chicago), as directed by your teacher. Books and online sources provide guidance for citing references. 
Plagiarize by copying others’ work without citation or by failing to cite references.

Source Evaluation Criteria 

A common test used to determine the validity of sources was developed by librarians at California State University, Chico. Their approach evaluates Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, and Purpose (CRAAP).

The CRAAP Test provides a list of questions to determine whether the information you’ve located is reliable. Please keep in mind that the following list is not static nor complete. The importance of the different criteria depend on your situation or need.

Currency: The timeliness of the information.

  • When was the information published or posted?
  • Has the information been revised or updated?
  • Is the information current or out of date with regard to your topic?
  • Are the links functional?

Relevance: The importance of the information for your needs. 

  • Does the information relate to your topic or answer your question?
  • Who is the intended audience?
  • Is the information at an appropriate level (not too elementary nor advanced for your needs)?
  • Have you reviewed a variety of sources before determining this is one that you will use?
  • Would you be comfortable using this source for a research paper?

Authority: The source of the information. 

  • Who is the author/publisher/source/sponsor?

  • What are the author’s credentials or organizational affiliations? Are they provided?

  • Is the author qualified to write on the topic?

  • Is there contact information, such as a publisher or email address?

  • Does the domain extension (.com, .edu, .gov, .org, .net) reveal anything about the author or source or lend credibility to the source?

  • Does the source properly credit original authors, data, images, and other intellectual property?

  • Does the source disclose whether emerging technologies (such as AI, data analytics, or automated content-generation tools) were used to create, analyze, or present the information? If so, does this affect the credibility, reliability, or authority of the source?

Accuracy: The reliability, truthfulness, and correctness of the informational content. 

  • Where does the information come from?

  • Is the information supported by evidence?

  • Has the information been reviewed or refereed?

  • Can you verify any of the information in another source or from personal knowledge?

  • Does the information conflict with findings from other credible sources? If so, what evidence supports each perspective?

  • Does the language or tone seem unbiased and free of emotion?

  • Are there spelling, grammar, or other typographical errors?

  • If emerging technologies (such as AI) were used to create or summarize the information, can the claims be verified through reputable independent sources?

Purpose: The reason the information exists. 

  • What is the purpose of the information? To inform? Teach? Sell? Entertain? Persuade?
  • Do the authors/sponsors make their intentions or purpose clear?
  • Is the information personal opinion? Propaganda?
  • Does the point of view appear objective and impartial?
  • Are there political, ideological, cultural, religious, institutional, or personal biases?
