Lesson 6.2 — Day 2
Ethics in
Artificial Intelligence
Building something that works is step one. Building something that should exist — or that behaves fairly in the real world — is a different challenge. Today we look at where AI goes wrong and what engineers are obligated to do about it.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to identify at least two real cases where AI bias caused harm; apply the engineering canons to an AI scenario; explain how shared accountability works; and describe what responsible AI practice looks like.

Slide 2 of 8
Why AI Raises Unique Issues
Scale

One Model, Millions of Decisions

A biased human judge affects one case at a time. A biased algorithm deployed in a courtroom, hospital, or bank affects every case simultaneously — at machine speed.

Opacity

The Black Box Problem

Large models make decisions through millions of learned weights. Even the engineers who built them can't fully explain why the model chose a particular output.

The hard question: If you can't fully explain a decision, and that decision affects someone's freedom, health, or livelihood — should you deploy it?
Slide 3 of 8
Real Cases of Biased AI
Case 1 — Criminal Justice

COMPAS Recidivism Tool

Used in US courts to predict whether someone would commit another crime. A ProPublica investigation found it flagged Black defendants as high risk at twice the rate of white defendants — even when both groups had similar actual reoffense rates.

Case 2 — Hiring

Amazon's Résumé Algorithm (2018)

Amazon trained a hiring tool on a decade of résumés — which reflected a historically male-dominated workforce. It learned to downgrade résumés that included the word "women's" and penalized graduates of all-women's colleges. Amazon scrapped the project.

Case 3 — Facial Recognition

Higher Error Rates for Darker Skin

MIT Media Lab found that three major commercial face-recognition systems had error rates up to 34% higher for dark-skinned women compared to light-skinned men. The gap traced directly to unbalanced training datasets.

Slide 4 of 8
Engineering Canons Applied to AI
The National Society of Professional Engineers sets fundamental canons. Each one applies directly to AI systems.
Canon 1
Hold public safety paramount. AI in health, criminal justice, or transportation must pass a safety bar before deployment — not after.
Canon 2
Act within areas of competence. Deploying a model you don't understand — and can't explain — likely exceeds your competence.
Canon 3
Be honest and objective. If testing reveals bias or failure modes, report them — even if it delays the project or reduces revenue.
Canon 4
Avoid deceptive acts. Do not present accuracy metrics in misleading ways (e.g., high overall accuracy that masks failure on specific groups).
Slide 5 of 8
The Trolley Problem — Now in Code
In philosophy, the trolley problem asks: is it acceptable to take an action that harms one person in order to save five? Self-driving cars face this — at 60 mph, with no time to ask a human.

The Classic Dilemma

An unavoidable crash: the car can hit five pedestrians, or swerve and hit one. The algorithm must choose. Whatever it does, engineers programmed that choice in advance.

Why This Is Harder Now

A human driver reacts instinctively and is judged morally. An autonomous vehicle's behavior is a policy decision made by engineers before the crash ever happens — affecting every car on the road.

The engineering decision doesn't just affect one car. It's written once, deployed in millions. That's why the ethics of autonomous systems are engineering decisions, not just philosophical puzzles.
Slide 6 of 8
Who Is Responsible When AI Causes Harm?
🔬
Designers
Chose the training data, the model architecture, and what to optimize for. Responsible for documented bias and known failure modes.
🏢
Deployers
Decided to put the system into production in a high-stakes context. Responsible for whether testing was adequate and for ongoing monitoring.
👤
Users
Acted on AI output without critical evaluation. Responsible for treating AI recommendations as gospel rather than as one input among many.
No one gets to say "the algorithm decided." Accountability is shared across the chain — which means engineers are not off the hook once the model ships.
Slide 7 of 8
What Responsible Engineers Actually Do
🧪
Test for disparate impact. Break accuracy down by demographic group, not just overall. A 95% accurate system may be 70% accurate for one group.
📝
Document limitations. Write down what the model is not good at, what data it was trained on, and what it should not be used for.
🚦
Set deployment gates. Define the conditions under which the system will NOT be released — and enforce them.
🔄
Monitor in production. Real-world performance often differs from test performance. Build feedback loops and watch for drift.
🗣️
Speak up. If you're on the team and you see a problem, you have an obligation under the engineering canons to flag it — even if you're not the decision maker.
Slide 8 of 8 — Recap
What We Covered
✓
AI raises unique ethical issues because of scale and opacity — one model, millions of decisions, limited explainability.
✓
Real systems (COMPAS, Amazon hiring, facial recognition) have caused measurable harm through biased training data.
✓
The engineering canons apply directly: safety paramount, honesty in reporting, acting within competence.
✓
Accountability is shared among designers, deployers, and users. "The algorithm did it" is not an excuse.
