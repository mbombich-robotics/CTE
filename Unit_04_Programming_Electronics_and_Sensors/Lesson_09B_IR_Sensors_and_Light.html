How IR Sensors See

Light, Reflection & Line Detection — Lesson 4.9B

Academic Standard 1E3 — Principles of Ray Optics

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Identify infrared (IR) light on the electromagnetic spectrum and explain how it differs from visible light
  • Describe reflection of light using the law of reflection — and explain why dark and light surfaces reflect differently
  • Explain how an IR line sensor uses emitted and reflected IR light to detect a surface boundary
  • Predict sensor output (high or low) based on the surface color beneath it
Why this matters: You have already wired and coded an IR line sensor. This lesson explains the physics behind why it works — so you can debug it when it behaves unexpectedly, and so you understand that your robot is doing real optics, not magic.

Infrared (IR) Light

  • Wavelength just longer than red visible light — your eyes cannot see it
  • You feel IR as heat — it is what a heat lamp radiates
  • Your TV remote uses IR to send signals
  • Night-vision cameras detect IR instead of visible light

Same Physics, Different Wavelength

  • IR obeys all the same laws as visible light: it travels in straight lines, reflects off surfaces, and can be absorbed or transmitted
  • Because it follows the same rules, we can use it for sensing — emit a beam, measure what comes back
  • Your L.E.O. line sensors emit IR at ~950 nm — just past the red edge of what you can see

Ray Optics: Reflection

Light bounces — but how much depends on the surface

Law of Reflection: The angle the incoming ray makes with the surface normal equals the angle the reflected ray makes. Every time, no exceptions.

High Reflectivity — Light Surfaces

  • White and light-colored surfaces reflect most of the incident IR back
  • The detector receives a strong return signal
  • Sensor output: HIGH

Low Reflectivity — Dark Surfaces

  • Dark surfaces absorb most of the incident IR — very little bounces back
  • The detector receives a weak or no return signal
  • Sensor output: LOW
This is not a design choice — it is physics. Dark pigments absorb more light across the spectrum, including IR.

Inside the IR Line Sensor

Two components, one job: measure reflected IR

IR LED — The Emitter

  • Continuously emits IR light downward toward the surface
  • Always on while the sensor is powered — you cannot "turn off" the emitter in code
  • Beam spreads slightly as it travels (not a perfect line)

Phototransistor — The Detector

  • Measures the intensity of reflected IR hitting it
  • More IR reflected → transistor conducts more → high voltage output
  • Less IR reflected → transistor conducts less → low voltage output
  • Your Pico reads this as a digital 1 or 0 on a GPIO pin

The Robot Reads the Line

Putting it together — optics into code

Sensor over LIGHT surface

  • Emitter fires IR downward
  • Light surface reflects most IR back
  • Detector receives strong signal → 1
  • Your code: "I'm off the line"

Sensor over DARK line

  • Emitter fires IR downward
  • Dark line absorbs most IR — very little returns
  • Detector receives weak signal → 0
  • Your code: "I'm on the line"
Why your sensor sometimes behaves oddly: Ambient light (room lighting, sunlight through windows) adds IR to the environment. If the background IR is strong enough, it can swamp the detector and make even a dark line look "bright." This is why shiny floors or direct sunlight can confuse your line follower — it's not a code bug, it's physics.
Connection to Standard 1E3: "Use the principles of ray optics to describe reflection and refraction of light." Your IR line sensor is a direct application of reflection: the emitter produces a ray, the surface reflects it according to its optical properties, and the detector measures what returns. This is ray optics doing real work in an engineering system.
