Building Effective Presentations

Scientists and engineers are often asked to present their work for a variety of purposes, including sharing ideas with colleagues, sharing information with the general public, and communicating findings with others in their field. Building an effective presentation means knowing who your audience is, constructing engaging content, and planning an organized delivery.    

Know the Audience

Who is your audience and what do they care about? Use the following prompts to help you identify the characteristics, needs, and wants of your target audience. 

  • Consider your primary audience:

    • What does the audience already know about the topic?
    • What is their level of technical expertise? (highly technical? semi-technical? nontechnical?)
    • What does the audience need or want from the presentation? (information? motivation? entertainment?)
    • What is the audience interested in, and how can you make connections to those interests? Examples:
      • A general, high-school-student audience might be interested in their friends, school activities, new technologies, social media, or an approaching school function. 
      • An environmental science class might be interested in global change, food waste at the school, a class’s community garden, or a topic they are currently studying.

Construct the Content

You may already know a lot about the topic, or you might have to research the topic to gather information, but having information is only the beginning of developing content for a presentation. You will not be able to share everything there is to know about a topic, so how do you decide what to include in your presentation? 

  • Use the following prompts to guide your content selection.

    • What is the purpose of the presentation?
      • Are you providing information?
      • Are you persuading or motivating the audience to take action?
    • What content is required to achieve the purpose?
      • What are the must-haves?
    • What content will help engage the audience around the purpose?
      • How can you grab the audience’s attention?
      • What is interesting and relevant to the audience?
      • What is needed to provide clarity to the audience?
      • What is not important and should be left out?

Organize the Delivery

After identifying the audience and determining what you need to say (and not say), you are ready to write a draft of your presentation. Remember to build a presentation that is easy for listeners to follow. An effective presentation contains the following elements. 

Engaging Opening

Give the audience something to pique their interest—a challenge, a surprising fact, meaningful quote, or brief emotional story—that is relevant to the topic.

Organized Body

Select an organizational structure that makes sense for the purpose of the topic. The following are some options for organizational structure, but there are others. 

Problem: Getting medicine to remote locations
Solution: Using drones to deliver medications

COVID-19 mRNA Vaccinations vs. COVID-19 Viral Vector Vaccinations

The best way to treat the flu...
While getting the yearly flu vaccine is the best step in decreasing the severity of the flu, some antivirals, like Tamiflu, can also be key. Also of note, are a few home remedies that help alleviate the intensity of flu symptoms.

The steps of a lab.

Powerful Closing

Leave the listener with something to remember. You can briefly summarize your presentation, provide a memorable idea, or motivate them to take action. Some of the same approaches that make an engaging opening may also work as a powerful closing. 

Example Presentation Overview

A student is tasked with presenting for their Biomedical Science class about engineering a better medicine. The student is proposing that federal and state health officials need to dedicate more resources to create a vaccine for Acute Flaccid Myelitis, known as AFM, which affects the body’s nervous system—specifically, the spinal cord—and can cause paralysis.

How Would You Build Your Presentation?

Teacher, students, representatives from hospitals, pharmaceutical companies, and government health offices.

Persuade the stakeholders of the importance of dedicating resources—like time, money, people, equipment, and information to finding a solution that will eliminate AFM. 

In this example, we are trying to ask for resources (research and funding), therefore we want the audience to understand the importance of this disease. To help them do this, we would want to include the following: 

  • Symptoms that children face - This can help show the severity of the disease and what life with it is like for these children. It can not only bring awareness, but can tug at the heartstrings of those we are asking to invest.
  • What the current treatment options are 
  • How many children are infected - Using numbers and percentages can help show the need for research. If an illness affects a large part of the population or has an infection rate that is on the rise (such as AFM), it shows a much greater need.
  • What the recovery rate is
  • What type of recovery is expected (full or partial) - This can lead back to the type of life the children are expected to have. If they do not fully recover, what are the lasting effects of having this disease and how will that affect their future? Will they be able to attend school? Play sports? Hold a job? This could also be a good place to include the mortality rate.
  • How to prevent exposure - This is where you can hit the main point about the need for a vaccine.

Some of the information from the required content can be used to engage the audience. An engaging presentation can keep the audience (and in this example, investors) thinking about your presentation long after it’s over. 


In the case of AFM, we may want to bring attention to the following statistics:

  • AFM has polio-like symptoms. Before its eradication in the US, polio was a devastating disease that paralyzed or killed over half a million people worldwide each year in the 1940s and 50s. Reminding the audience of an endemic such as polio through numbers or visuals can grab their attention and spark the correlation between AFM and Polio, setting up AFM as a devastating disease that has a real need.
  • The disease affects children - It is human nature to want to have a full life and grow old. When we see diseases cut that life short or change the quality of it, especially in children, we are naturally more inclined to act and help. 
  • The cause is unknown - This is another reason that we need the audience’s help with research. Until we know what causes it, we cannot prevent it. Without research, we won’t be able to answer the critical question of whether the solution should be a vaccine or something else.

Engaging Opener Example: 


Is my child having a stroke? While the answer to that may be No, the symptoms for Acute Flaccid Myelitis are the same and just as dangerous.  


Body Organization Example - Problem/Solution:


The incidence of this disease is on the rise in the United States and there IS something you can do about it. Acute Flaccid Myelitis is caused by a virus and with your help, we can create a vaccine so no child or their family has to suffer from this deadly disease. 


Powerful Closing Example: 


Don’t let the number of children who suffer from AFM end up equalling that of polio. Let’s research and vaccinate our children to give them the bright future they deserve.
