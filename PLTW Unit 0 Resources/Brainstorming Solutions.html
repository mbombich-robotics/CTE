Brainstorming Solutions

Brainstorming is a technique that allows for the free-flowing share of ideas and creative thinking. 

TermDefinition
brainstormingA group technique for solving problems, generating ideas, stimulating creative thinking, by unrestrained spontaneous participation in discussion. 

Brainstorming can be: completed individually or in groups, structured or free form, and timed or untimed. Most importantly, brainstorming involves collecting ideas without regard to feasibility and without criticism. 

  • Basic Brainstorming Rules

    • Capture all ideas.

    • Allow all members to contribute.

    • Criticism is not allowed.

    • Work for quantity to allow quality ideas to surface.

    • Welcome piling on. Use others’ ideas as launch pads. 

    • Allow a free-for-all.

    • Encourage big thinking.

Recording Ideas

Be sure to capture all ideas during a brainstorming session. Designate a good notetaker who can compile all notes from the session. This might include taking photographs of whiteboards or notebook pages. You can use a variety of methods to record your ideas. 

Brainstorming Techniques

You will be asked to generate ideas—to brainstorm—in a variety of activities, projects, and problems throughout your PLTW courses. The following techniques and tips can help you get started with brainstorming strategies.

  • Forced Association. Creating new ideas by mentally forcing the association of two seemingly unrelated items. An example is Johan Gutenberg’s idea for the printing press. He combined the ideas of a wine press and a coin stamp to think of a movable-type press.

  • SCAMMPERR. Starting with a current solution, use the acronym SCAMMPERR to generate new and different ideas or approaches (Figure 1). 

  • Mind Mapping. Using a graphical representation to generate, visualize, and organize ideas to solve a problem. Ideas are arranged in a branching fashion according to relative importance, with the major ideas branching directly from the center main idea. Other ideas that are triggered by or related to a major idea are then connected to that major idea. You can use lines, symbols, words, color, and images to organize and represent your thoughts in a mind map (Figure 2). 

Brainstorming Exercises and Sample Prompts

Brainstorming prompts are offered throughout PLTW courses as they relate to various project descriptions. The prompts below are more generic to be used as brainstorming exercises.

Bad Ideas FirstThe best brainstorming occurs when everyone feels comfortable throwing out all of their ideas, regardless of how feasible they are. Boost confidence and help the tone of the room by spending the first 10 minutes of a brainstorming session generating bad ideas first. How can we get kids to play outside more? 
Word GamesTo help stir up some creative thinking, write down one word, and then have students brainstorm any other words that come to mind from that first word. Encourage students to think about the function of the word, the senses the word evokes, and metaphors that can be associated with it. Avoid Brainstorming Blockades

The following scenarios can result in poor brainstorming sessions:  

  • Using a poor design brief.
  • Assuming there is only one right answer.
  • Getting hooked on the first solution.  
  • Considering the ideas from only one or two team members.
  • Feeling too anxious to finish a project.
  • Becoming frustrated by the lack of success.
  • Getting hooked on a solution that almost works.

Additional Information

Researchers at Stanford University conducted four experiments to find out whether walking boosts creative ideation. The researchers found that creativity was substantially enhanced while walking. Additionally, the creative benefits continued for a short time even after the participants sat down after walking. Walking inside on a treadmill and outside both generated creative boosts. But according to study results, “walking outside produced the most novel and highest quality analogies.”

  • View the TED Talk, “Want to be more creative? Go for a Walk” presented by Marily Oppesso.
  • Read the article, “Give Your Ideas Some Legs: The Positive Effect of Walking on Creative Thinking” (Marily Oppezzo and Daniel L. Schwartz, Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, 2014).

If You Get Stuck...

  • Combine promising partial solutions.
  • Try a different brainstorming technique.
  • Try both individual and group brainstorming.
  • Do more research.
    • Consider ideas used in other scientific products or processes. 
    • Consider ideas used in unrelated products or processes.
